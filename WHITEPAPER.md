# GCSC White Paper — Global Construction Smart Contract

## Status of this document

This white paper describes a product vision and planned capabilities. It is not
a claim that every feature described here is available today. The pilot is
planned to begin in Washington State, and individual capabilities will be
tested progressively. Specific features will depend on published availability
and terms. This document is general planning information, not legal, insurance,
lending, engineering, or permit advice for a particular property. Confirm
requirements and contract questions with the relevant qualified professionals.

## 1. The problem: a fragmented remodeling journey

A homeowner who wants to remodel a kitchen, finish a basement, or add a room
faces a scattered process: contractors on one site, windows on another,
financing somewhere else, reviews on yet another site, and the latest estimate
lost among messages on a phone. There is no clear starting point and no single
place where decisions, documents, and evidence of completed work stay
connected.

GCSC — Global Construction Smart Contract — is being designed to bring that
fragmented process into one project workspace: from the first photograph and
contractor selection to materials, financing, staged payments, and checking
the result. The central idea is straightforward: homeowners keep their
choice, contractors receive a clearer brief, and decisions, documents, and
evidence stay connected.

## 2. The product: one project workspace

The workspace is intended to hold, for a single project: the homeowner's
request, selected contractors, proposals, materials, documents, messages,
stages, and completion evidence. The combination is the point: search,
communication, materials, documents, financing, and oversight support each
other, instead of living in disconnected tools.

## 3. Contractor selection starts with the homeowner

The platform should not push one company on a homeowner simply because it
paid for promotion. GCSC envisions two routes: the homeowner chooses suitable
contractors and invites them to discuss the project, or publishes a request
so interested contractors can respond.

Filters under consideration include specialty, service area, availability,
budget fit, and verifiable experience with comparable projects. A contractor
profile is intended to offer more than an attractive cover image: company
history and how much of it is verifiable, comparable completed projects,
customer reviews with the work concerned, posting date, and whether the
reviewer's project participation was verified, relevant documents and
qualifications with the status and currency of checks made clear, and working
arrangements such as availability, proposed timing, and project-management
approach.

The homeowner could send several companies the same clear request — scope,
photographs, and preferred timing — so that proposals are compared for the
same brief rather than for three different scopes. Business registration
should not be presented as proof of craftsmanship: in Washington, general
contractor registration is distinct from a competency test, and separate
requirements apply to certain trades.

## 4. From photographs to a structured request, with AI assistance

Homeowners should not have to know construction terminology to get started.
Under the GCSC concept, they could begin with photographs and a plain-language
explanation. An AI assistant would ask follow-up questions — intended use,
desired result, known problems, approximate budget, and timing — and prepare
a structured draft request, also called a job lead, combining work location,
project type, photographs, preferences, constraints, questions for the
contractor, and a preliminary list of issues to verify on site. The homeowner
would review and edit the draft before approving it for sending or
publication.

AI must not turn an assumption into an established fact. Photographs cannot
reliably reveal every hidden defect, establish structural safety, or replace
an on-site assessment. The assistant's role is to frame the task and identify
missing information, not to declare a final price or construction method
without verification. For contractors, the result is a request with fewer
guesses and more useful starting information.

## 5. Proposals, direct communication, and lead access

GCSC plans to let the homeowner compare responses and communicate directly
with contractors on the platform. Questions, photographs, scope
clarifications, estimate versions, and approvals should remain linked to the
project. Contractors could study the request, ask questions, prepare a
proposal, and use AI tools to organize information and draft calculations
where appropriate. The final proposal and professional responsibility for it
remain with the contractor.

Contractors need projects that match their specialty, geography, team
capacity, and schedule. Planned filters would let them choose requests by
city and service area, work category, budget, preferred start, and project
size, and exclude unsuitable work. Depending on the chosen marketplace model,
a contractor could respond to a posted project, purchase access to a suitable
lead, or receive a direct invitation from a homeowner. Buying access to a
lead must not mean buying the customer: homeowners retain the ability to
assess a company, reject an unsuitable proposal, and choose someone else, and
a remodeling contract follows agreement between the parties, not
automatically from a lead-access payment. Even a well-matched lead does not
guarantee a signed contract.

Before a construction contract is signed, the proposed system would allow
the homeowner to withdraw the request, change the search approach, or choose
another contractor. For a contractor who has already paid for lead access,
the founder is considering a refund of most of the fee, with a defined amount
retained for processing or access already provided. The amounts, timing, and
eligibility rules are not finalized, and neither a particular refund
percentage nor an unconditional full refund should be promised until those
rules are approved. Withdrawing a lead is different from ending an existing
remodeling contract, where obligations and dispute processes depend on the
agreement and applicable requirements.

## 6. Materials and manufacturers: a construction search hub

Choosing a contractor solves only part of the problem. GCSC's vision
includes an organized construction catalog — a useful place to begin product
research, compare options, and prepare decisions, rather than collect
information from dozens of disconnected websites. The plan is to organize
information by product category and manufacturer, with listings covering
specifications, available options, intended applications, supplier
information, reviews, and video reviews where available.

Supply geography is part of the idea: if a desired product is manufactured
outside Washington, in another state or country, the homeowner should be able
to understand whom to order through, whether supply to their area is
available, and what to ask about delivery, timing, and service. Coverage,
current product information, and supply terms would depend on the database's
development and supplier participation, and not every manufacturer worldwide
should be assumed listed at launch. A selected product could then be
discussed in its project context: whether it fits, when it can arrive, who
will install it, and how changing the selection affects the estimate.

## 7. An AI guide through the decisions

For someone undertaking a major remodel for the first time, even the sequence
can be confusing: find a contractor or select materials first, when to
resolve permit questions, what belongs in an estimate, what must happen
before the next payment. GCSC's planned AI consultant would help guide that
sequence: explain terms, ask clarifying questions, help prepare for
contractor conversations, and show which decisions are still open.

The assistant would not have authority to quietly accept a contract, change
the budget, or move money on the homeowner's behalf. It supports decisions;
the necessary approvals remain with the homeowner, contractor, and relevant
professionals. A well-organized service should reduce confusion and help the
homeowner understand the work while keeping them in control — not promise
that the homeowner never needs to participate.

## 8. Exploring financing before work begins

An important GCSC goal is to help homeowners explore financing before
construction starts. The concept covers remodeling, additions, reconstruction,
and a clearly defined remaining scope of unfinished work, rather than only
reimbursement after costs have already been incurred. The proposed process
begins with a prepared project — scope, selected or prospective contractor,
estimate, schedule, materials requirements, and milestone plan — that could
help present the project to suitable financing partners.

The longer-term concept also considers the home's anticipated condition and
value after renovation. An AI projection is not a formal appraisal, and a
prepared plan is not automatic loan approval. Financing availability, amount,
security, interest rate, and other conditions would depend on the product,
borrower, and property assessment, and the authorized partner's decision.
Borrowed funds are not free money: repayment obligations, possible interest,
security, and budget changes need to be considered before signing. The honest
promise is therefore not "risk-free remodeling" but better project
preparation, an opportunity to explore financing early, and a clearer
connection between spending and the agreed work plan.

## 9. Stage payments: materials and completed work accounted for separately

Handing over the entire budget and hoping for a good result should not be
the foundation of trust. GCSC's model envisions agreed milestones with clear
evidence and payment conditions. Materials and labor could be tracked
separately: a payment allocated to particular materials would connect to an
agreed list, supplier order, invoices, and other evidence of its use, while
for labor, a defined amount of completed, accepted work would correspond to
a defined payment — a milestone covering preparation, a finished section, or
another agreed portion of the project.

With a suitable operating model, that could allow frequent payments,
including daily payments for work that has actually been confirmed, giving a
reliable contractor more predictable cash flow while the homeowner does not
prepay a large scope that still exists only as a promise. Payments would
follow required evidence and approvals, considering the contract, any
dispute, and financing-partner requirements. AI could help check
completeness and make a recommendation, but one uploaded photograph should
not automatically remove every safeguard and release money, and a materials
label with a receipt photograph alone cannot prevent misuse: how tightly
spending can be restricted would depend on the payment mechanism, partners,
and final contractual rules. Retaining part of a payment until a milestone or
correction is completed would also require clear advance rules, so that
homeowner protection does not become an unjustified refusal to pay for
properly completed work.

## 10. Following progress with a phone and AI support

Project oversight should not require the homeowner to stay on site constantly
and manually collect every document. GCSC envisions photographs, video,
contractor reports, and other evidence added from a phone. The workspace
should show the current planned stage, what the contractor reports as
completed, the evidence supplied, and questions still open, so that comparing
that information with the schedule could reveal delays earlier. AI could help
compare a report with the agreed scope, identify missing evidence, and flag
inconsistencies — exposing a gap rather than creating an impression of
complete oversight when a required document or agreed photograph is missing.

A phone and an algorithm cannot see everything. Concealed construction,
installation methods, and some defects require qualified on-site review, and
mandatory inspections cannot be replaced by AI assessment. The value is
earlier questions and a clearer project history, which reduces uncertainty
without turning remote monitoring into an infallible guarantee.

## 11. Optional protection: independent assessment and correction

A contractor's bond, insurance, or warranty does not mean every dispute will
automatically result in a quick, free repair. The founder therefore proposes
an optional project-protection program that a homeowner could purchase
separately. A preliminary price idea is about $100 per project, but the final
price, eligibility, coverage, and legal structure are not approved, and the
program cannot currently be sold as an available $100 insurance policy with
an unconditional promise to redo any remodel.

The idea goes beyond compensation: an organized path from a specific concern
to an independent assessment and, where the claim qualifies, correction of
the work. The homeowner would identify the departure from the agreement or
other supported concern and provide evidence. The model under consideration
could involve two independent qualified specialists or contractors with
relevant expertise visiting the site, inspecting the work, comparing it with
the scope and applicable requirements, recording their conclusions, and
determining whether a correction is needed. This is a separate, paid
professional assessment, not an ordinary free estimate intended to sell
another remodel, and the assessors' payment should not depend on their
finding a defect.

If a qualifying defect is confirmed, the founder's concept is for GCSC to
organize and pay for an agreed correction by another suitable contractor.
Funding might come from the protection program and, where the contract
allows, lawfully retained payment owed to the original contractor — which
does not give the platform a right to seize money based solely on a
homeowner's allegation. Repairs could be carried out by a different
contractor or, if future rules allow, one of the assessors, which would
require transparent conflict-of-interest disclosure, separate approval, and
verification of the result. Before launch, the program would need defined
limits, exclusions, eligible project types, funding, an appeal process, and
any necessary authorization or partners. The intended result is practical: a
homeowner with a confirmed problem should have a defined route to
professional consideration and resolution, rather than a dead end of
unanswered calls.

## 12. A reputation people can examine, not buy

The shared foundation is a verifiable history: reviews connected to real
projects, with contractor performance assessed through more than an
advertising description. The proposed platform would not sell positive
ratings or quietly erase justified criticism to improve a profile, though it
would still need rules for replies, factual corrections, privacy, and
disputed content.

GCSC's longer-term vision includes blockchain-supported records as a way to
make changes more visible. That is a planned direction, not an already
operational system, and the current version of the project works without
blockchain. A blockchain does not establish whether a photograph is honest
or whether work was installed correctly; its possible role is the integrity
of the history, while professional assessment, privacy, and a clear
correction process remain necessary.

## 13. How the pieces work together, and what comes next

Imagine a homeowner wanting to turn an unfinished basement into a home
office: they upload photographs, explain the goal to an AI assistant, and
review a structured draft request. They select contractors by experience,
service area, and reviews, or publish a lead for suitable businesses. In one
workspace, they receive responses, clarify proposals, explore materials, and
compare options. If financing is needed, the prepared scope, estimate, and
schedule could support a partner's review before work begins. Once
agreements are in place, the project is divided into stages: purchases have
supporting records, progress is documented by phone, and payments are linked
to accepted work. If a quality concern arises, the original agreements,
photographs, and change history remain available, and if the project
participates in a future protection program, its terms would govern the
concern, including independent assessment and any qualifying correction.

No platform can honestly promise a remodel with no risk, no homeowner
decisions, and no possibility of disagreement. A more useful goal is to
reduce blind payments, repeated searches, and disputes without records or a
clear process. That is the direction of Global Construction Smart Contract:
the homeowner chooses the contractor, the contractor receives a clearer
task, materials and proposals stay with the project, and payments, evidence,
and responsibility connect to agreed stages. Washington is the intended
starting point, with the model and individual capabilities tested
progressively.
